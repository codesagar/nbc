Arrival in India

10 minute read · 36 illustrations

Neet and Buddy’s adventure in India began the moment they stepped out of the airport. A horn sounded before Neet had decided whom to bark at. By the time he answered it, three more had joined in. Neet, the fiery orange chihuahua, immediately puffed out his chest, ready to take on this new land. Buddy, his dachshund-border terrier mix brother, stayed close enough for Heather’s bag to brush his back.

Heather hailed a taxi, loading the crates and luggage into the small, bright yellow car. Buddy watched his favorite blanket disappear beneath a suitcase. He had accompanied it across an ocean. It was now traveling without him.

He stretched toward the luggage until Heather drew him onto the seat beside her.

“It’s coming too,” she told him, showing him the corner still poking out. “Nobody gets left at the airport.”

Buddy settled against her. Neet was already inspecting the window.

As the vehicle weaved through traffic, Neet pressed his nose against the glass, his Dorito-shaped ears twitching with excitement.

“Look, Buddy! Tuk-tuks! Cows! So many bikes! It’s like a giant obstacle course!” he exclaimed, his tiny paws scrambling for a better view.

Buddy peeked out from under Heather’s arm. “I don’t know, Neet. It’s… loud.”

“Loud? This is life, Buddy! Adventure waits for no dog!” Neet declared, his voice barely audible over the horns and shouting vendors.

Buddy waited for him to finish. “What?”

Neet took a breath to repeat the speech. The taxi honked. Buddy settled back under Heather’s arm.

Their first stop was Heather’s family home in a small village. The air smelled of spices, flowers, and something Neet decided was “deliciously dangerous.” Heather set their crates in the courtyard, brought in the luggage and latched the gate. Buddy could see his blanket in the open suitcase. Before he could reach it, Neet planted himself between the bags.

“Nobody moves anything until I’ve checked it.”

Buddy waited while Neet sniffed a wheel. Then another wheel. There were four.

He went around the other side, took the blanket corner in his mouth and backed away. A sandal slid off the luggage and landed on the blanket. Buddy kept pulling. Whatever this place required, apparently he had brought it.

“Foreign footwear!” Neet announced.

Heather picked up her sandal. “That’s mine.”

The inspection became more delicate. Neet sniffed it again before permitting Heather to put it on.

Buddy dragged his blanket into a strip of shade beside the house. He turned twice, tucked his paws underneath him and let his chin sink into its familiar folds.

A group of curious street dogs gathered outside the gate.

Neet immediately sprang into action.

“Stay back, you scoundrels! This is my territory now!” he barked from the courtyard.

The street dogs tilted their heads, more amused than threatened. One particularly scruffy dog stepped forward, wagging his tail. “Relax, little one. We’re just saying hello.”

Buddy lifted his head. His blanket was warm, the shade was pleasant, and the loudest thing in the village was now inside the gate with him.

“Maybe let him say hello first?”

“They need to know who’s boss first!” Neet replied, standing as tall as his four-pound frame would allow.

The scruffy dog sat down to wait. Two others sat beside him.

Neet held his ground. The visitors seemed prepared to watch him do it all afternoon. Buddy left his blanket, edged out from behind Heather and offered the scruffy dog a small, cautious wag. It was answered at once.

“See?” Buddy said. “That was hello.”

“I hadn’t finished the warning.”

“Is it quieter inside?” Buddy asked the scruffy dog.

“Usually.”

Both of them looked at Neet.

Buddy went back for his blanket. He pulled it toward the doorway, intending to demonstrate where they could all stop talking. Neet immediately trotted alongside.

“Where are you moving the border?”

“Inside.”

“Then they’ll think they won.”

Buddy stopped with the blanket halfway over the threshold. Neet stood on the trailing corner to prevent any further retreat. The scruffy dog lay down outside the gate. He seemed comfortable with the border exactly where it was.

Heather laughed, scooping Neet up before his self-appointed diplomatic mission could escalate.

“You can meet the neighbors after you’ve had a drink.” She carried him inside, then bent to help Buddy bring the blanket over the step.

A cool tiled floor and bowls of water awaited them. Buddy drank, stretched across a patch of tile, and discovered he had transported his blanket several thousand miles to lie beside it.

Neet drank noisily, paused to check the doorway, then drank again. Taking charge was thirsty work.

Heather sat down long enough to rub Buddy’s ears and let Neet examine her other sandal. By evening, Buddy had found a place beside her chair where the tiles stayed cool. Neet could watch the gate from there. Buddy could watch Heather. For a while, neither needed to move.

The next day, Heather decided to take them on a rickshaw ride to the local market. Buddy watched her fold his blanket and place it beside the window.

“That’s your spot when we get back,” she said, smoothing one rumpled edge.

Buddy nosed it once. Then he followed her to the door. A reserved place at home was reassuring. Having Heather within reach was better.

She clipped on their leads before they left. Neet climbed into the colorful three-wheeler before Buddy had finished examining the step. Heather helped Buddy up and sat between them, gathering the leads close.

The moment the rickshaw jerked forward, Neet stood on his hind legs, barking instructions to the driver.

“Faster! Left turn! Watch out for that cow!” he yapped, his Dorito ears flapping in the breeze.

The driver went right. Neet swayed left, landed back on the seat and immediately barked, “Right!”

Buddy clung to Heather, his wide eyes darting from side to side. “This thing doesn’t even have doors!”

Heather drew him closer. He tucked himself firmly between her and the seat back. Neet could have the view. Buddy had secured a corner that did not open onto a cow.

At the next stop, the driver glanced back and grinned at his noisy passenger. Neet took the grin as a request for further instructions.

“Clear ahead!”

The cow stepped in front of the rickshaw.

They waited.

Buddy rested his chin on Heather’s arm. “Ask it to go right.”

Neet studied the cow. It had not looked at him once.

“We’re letting it inspect the road.”

At the market, Neet sniffed at piles of spices, barked at hanging fabrics that fluttered in the wind, and tried to climb into a cart full of mangoes. Heather shortened his lead before his front paws reached the fruit.

Buddy chose her shaded side. Whenever Neet tried to leave it, Heather paused to bring them together again. Buddy began checking the route before they moved: open ground, Heather’s shoes, shade. He could do this.

Then a strip of blue cloth brushed his ear.

He stopped so abruptly that Heather stopped too. He inspected the cloth. He inspected the gap around it. He went the long way.

Neet, still looking at the mango cart, glanced over. “It’s cloth.”

“And those are fruit,” Buddy said.

Beyond the fabric stall was a narrow, shaded space beside stacked baskets. Buddy slipped toward it, pleased to have found somewhere that seemed to belong to nobody.

The cloth fluttered into his face again.

He backed out. The shade was excellent. The ceiling kept touching him.

Heather crouched and moved the hanging end aside so he could see through the passage. At the far end stood a red basket; beyond it, sunlight and the open lane.

Buddy walked through with her. Nothing touched his ears.

“There we go,” she said. She stroked his cheek, then rose to follow Neet’s determined interest in the neighboring fruit stall.

Buddy looked back at the red basket. He knew how to leave now. That made staying easier.

Their most memorable encounter came when a mischievous monkey perched on a nearby tree took an immediate interest in Neet. It swung down, chattering and pointing at the bold chihuahua. Neet, of course, saw this as a challenge.

“What do you want, you overgrown squirrel?” he barked, his tiny body bouncing on the spot.

The monkey snatched a mango and tossed it in Neet’s direction. Neet dodged expertly, his Dorito-shaped ears twitching with adrenaline.

“Is that all you’ve got?”

There was an entire cart of mangoes beside the monkey. Buddy looked at it, then at Neet.

The mango rolled against a basket. Buddy yelped and ducked behind Heather. The monkey reached for another.

Buddy could see the passage and, beyond it, the red basket. He tugged toward the clear end of the stall.

Neet lunged the other way. Heather held both leads steady. For a moment, Buddy was leading an expedition made almost entirely of people who would not follow him.

“This way,” he told Neet.

“He started it!”

The blue cloth swung across the passage. Buddy’s paws stopped. Then he lowered his head and stepped underneath it, keeping his lead taut enough for Heather to feel the direction.

He gave one more determined tug, planting his paws until she turned with him.

Heather stepped in, shooing the monkey away and scooping Neet into her arms. The fruit seller waved it back toward the tree. Heather picked up the fallen mango, returned it to him with a grateful nod, and followed Buddy out.

“You’re going to start an international incident,” she muttered, laughing as Neet wagged his tail triumphantly.

“He’s retreating!” Neet called over her arm.

Buddy kept walking. “So are we. Try to enjoy it.”

Past the red basket, he stopped beside Heather’s shoes. Neet peered back toward the stall, then at the quiet patch of lane around them.

“Good flanking move.”

Buddy shook the cloth tickle out of his ears. “We’re going home.”

Heather checked his lead, rubbed his back and hailed their ride. This time Buddy climbed in without inspecting the step. He knew where he wanted to sit.

By the time they returned home, both dogs were exhausted. Buddy collapsed onto his favorite blanket. Neet, however, sat by the window, staring out at the bustling street.

“This place is amazing, Buddy,” Neet said softly. “So much to see, so much to do. Tomorrow, we’ll conquer even more.”

Buddy sighed, his eyes closing. “As long as it’s not another monkey.”

Heather drew the curtain partway across the window. Neet moved his nose into the remaining gap. Buddy put his head beneath the blanket.

“You’ll miss everything,” Neet told him.

“That’s the plan.”

A horn sounded outside. Neet’s ears lifted.

“Tomorrow,” Heather said, rubbing the top of his head.

He stayed by the window, but leaned into her hand. Beneath the blanket, Buddy let out a long, satisfied sigh. For once, the expedition had stopped where he wanted.
