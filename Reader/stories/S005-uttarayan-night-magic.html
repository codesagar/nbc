Uttarayan - Night Magic

10 minute read · 33 illustrations

On the second evening of Uttarayan, Buddy brought his blanket closer to Heather’s chair. He had found a way to watch the sky without having to watch Neet as well. He took a corner in his teeth and pulled. Nothing happened. He pulled harder, then looked down at the two front paws with which he was firmly holding his own blanket in place.

Buddy stepped off, moved it one careful pace and lay down as though this had all been part of the arrangement. On Meera’s terrace, the kite spools were finally still.

Heather’s chair stood a little way inside the low terrace wall. Beside it sat a water bowl, a small table and the remains of a day in which Neet had personally supervised almost everything above Ahmedabad. Meera had put the kite string away. Neet had watched her do it with the grave expression of someone whose equipment was being confiscated.

Now there was nothing left to chase.

Then a light rose above the rooftops.

Neet stood up, his Dorito-shaped ears twitching. "Look at the sky, Buddy! It’s like the stars are coming down to play!"

Buddy lifted his head. "They’re not stars, Neet. Those are kites with lanterns."

Heather smiled, sipping her chai as she pointed to one of the glowing orbs. "It’s called a ‘tukkal.’ People attach small lanterns to their kites so they can fly them at night. Isn’t it beautiful?"

Neet watched the glowing kites drift higher. "Like fireflies," he said. "But much better organized."

One drifted left. Neet took two steps left. It drifted right. He took two steps right.

"You can watch them sitting down," Buddy said.

"Then who’s following that one?"

Buddy settled his chin on the blanket. He could see Heather’s hand, the sky and both of the feet currently moving toward him. He waited.

Neet stepped across the blanket without looking down.

Buddy raised his head just in time to avoid receiving a foot in his beard.

"There’s a whole terrace."

"This is where it’s going."

"The kite or your foot?"

Neet looked down. Buddy looked up. For a moment they shared the same extremely small piece of sky.

Heather gently steered Neet around the blanket with her ankle. "Mind your brother, high-flyer."

He went around. The lantern went left again. Neet completed a very important semicircle.

The terrace next door lit up as a group of neighbors began setting off small fireworks. Sparklers hissed and crackled, sending golden showers into the night. Neet yipped excitedly, running in circles. "Fireworks! Oh, Buddy, look! It’s like the sky is cheering for us!"

Buddy tucked himself closer to Heather. A rocket burst green and silver overhead. He liked the silver. He would have preferred it without the bang.

He pulled the blanket beneath her chair and crawled after it. The chair legs made a reassuring little enclosure. Neet could continue his circles elsewhere.

Buddy looked up.

He could see the underside of Heather.

He shuffled backward until his nose emerged. Beyond the terrace wall, a lantern swayed into view. He stretched his neck another inch. A firework cracked, and his nose withdrew.

Heather felt the blanket slide under her shoes and leaned forward. "Found a little house?"

Buddy wagged once. It was an excellent house with a disappointing sky.

Neet poked his head between two chair legs. "You’re missing everything."

"I know. I built a roof."

"That’s Heather."

Buddy rested his chin. At least it was a dependable roof.

But a gold light crossed the narrow gap beside her foot, and he wanted to see where it went. He eased forward again, taking the blanket with him, until half of him was sheltered and half could see. Heather lowered a hand to his back.

"It’s a little loud, but... it is kind of pretty," he admitted.

Neet had discovered a second sky.

It was smaller than the first and considerably easier to reach. The water bowl beside Heather’s chair held one wavering golden light. He leaned over it. The light leaned toward him.

"Buddy. One’s landed."

Buddy looked at the bowl from under Heather’s hand. "It’s a reflection."

Neet moved his head sideways. The light slipped sideways too.

"It’s avoiding me."

"Yes. In water."

Neet lowered his nose. The gold broke into ripples, and he jerked back with a drop hanging from his chin.

He stared at the bowl. Somewhere up there, a lantern was continuing its evening without the slightest concern for his wet nose.

"It broke."

"Try leaving it alone."

Neet waited. The light gathered itself again.

This was an unusually resilient opponent.

He tried to inspect it from the other side. To do so, he squeezed between the bowl and the table leg. He set one forepaw against the rim instead of the tile. The bowl tipped, rocked once and spilled a shallow stream across the tiles.

Buddy stood up. The stream arrived at his blanket.

He picked up a dry corner and backed away, pulling his careful arrangement out from beneath Heather’s feet. She put down her chai and caught the bowl before Neet could investigate the water now spreading beneath it.

"Well. That one really did come down to earth."

Neet shook his wet chin. Buddy looked at the dark patch growing on his blanket.

"You’ve put the sky in my bed."

Heather lifted the wet end. Buddy held the other corner for another second, determined to save what remained of his evening, then let her take it.

"Good catch, sweetheart. Let’s dry this bit."

She draped the blanket over the back of her chair with the wet edge hanging clear of the seat. Meera, coming through the terrace doorway, stopped with her tray of pakoras.

"Have we begun washing the furniture?"

"Only the blanket. So far."

Meera set the tray on the table, fetched a cloth, wiped the spill and refilled the bowl. She set it behind the table, out of Neet’s route. He inspected the cleared tiles. The ground sky had disappeared entirely.

"I think you frightened it," he told Buddy.

Buddy was already searching for somewhere dry.

The doorway opened into a small, dim landing. He could still hear Heather, and the crack of the next firework was softer there. He settled just inside, leaving his nose in the terrace air.

It smelled of warm pakoras.

Meera stepped carefully around him and sat cross-legged near Heather. "The nights of Uttarayan are my favorite," she said. "There’s something magical about the way the whole city feels alive, even in the quiet."

A firework cracked next door. Meera waited for it to finish.

"The occasional quiet," she amended, and Heather nearly spilled her chai.

Buddy’s ears relaxed. He had finally found the occasional quiet.

Unfortunately, the tray was on the terrace. So was Heather. So was the sky. His refuge contained a stair and half of a slipper.

He watched Neet nose his way toward the pakoras. Then he looked back into the landing.

The slipper offered no opinion.

Buddy got up.

He went to Heather and slipped his nose beneath her resting hand. When she stroked him, he took a step toward the doorway. She reached after him. He returned, pressed his nose into her palm again, and stepped away.

"What have you found?"

He waited beside the doorway and looked back.

Heather stood. Neet abandoned the tray immediately.

"Are we leaving? But there are still lights!"

Buddy stayed where he was. He didn't want to leave. He wanted everything good to be a little closer together.

Heather looked from him to the chair and smiled. "Ah. A better seat?"

She moved her chair nearer the doorway, turning it so she could still see the lanterns above the wall. Meera set the tray aside and brought the little table. Buddy checked the gap beside Heather’s feet. From here, he could tuck into the sheltered landing or stretch his head out to watch.

Meera laid a dry towel on the tiles. Heather folded the dry half of his blanket over it, keeping the damp end on her chair back.

Buddy walked onto the blanket, turned once and lay down.

Meera lowered the tray onto the table.

Buddy raised his head again.

"Now that," he told Neet, "is organized."

Neet looked at the new position. There was a clear strip of terrace in front of them and a very good view of the sky. He walked the strip once, then came back and sat beside Heather’s chair. Buddy’s roof had become an observation post.

Heather laughed, breaking off a tiny piece of pakora for Neet. "Don’t get used to this, Neet. You’re already spoiled."

Buddy received a small, soft piece and nibbled it slowly, savoring the warmth and spices. "It’s nice," he murmured.

Neet had already finished his. He looked at Buddy’s.

"You’re missing a kite," Buddy said.

Neet’s head shot up. Buddy finished his pakora. By the time Neet looked back, there was only a crumb in his beard.

"Which kite?"

Buddy swallowed. "You found it."

Heather brushed the crumb from his beard. "Very peaceful down here, isn’t it, Buddy?"

Neet sniffed the blanket’s dry edge. Buddy lifted his head. For once, his brother wasn't looking past him at something in the sky.

Neet touched his nose to Buddy’s shoulder, then sat down just outside the blanket.

Buddy let him stay there. The landing was quieter, the food had arrived and his bed no longer contained a river. He could afford a little company.

As the night deepened, the wind carried the faint hum of music from distant terraces. Meera hummed along until she reached a part she didn't know. Heather supplied three confident notes. Neither of them knew what came next, and both laughed.

Neet settled beside Heather, his head resting on her lap as his wide eyes tracked every glowing light.

"Do you think the kites ever get tired?" he asked, his voice unusually soft.

Buddy, now half-asleep, mumbled, "Maybe. But they look happy up there."

Heather stroked Neet’s fur gently. "They’re like dreams, soaring as long as the wind carries them."

Neet’s tail wagged lazily at the thought. "Then I want to be a kite. I’d fly higher than anyone."

"You already do, Neet," Heather said with a smile. "You’re my little high-flyer."

Buddy opened one eye. "Meera’s down here with the tray."

Neet considered the height of the sky and the height of the pakoras. "I’d come down at sensible intervals."

The terrace grew quieter as the night wore on, but the magic lingered. A final burst of fireworks painted the sky with streaks of red and gold, their reflection shimmering in Buddy’s sleepy eyes. Neet gazed at the lanterns as they floated out of sight, one by one, until only the stars remained.

"This was the best day ever," Neet whispered, his voice carrying a rare hint of calm.

Heather drew both dogs close. Buddy shifted to make room on the dry part of his blanket. Neet stopped searching for the lanterns and closed his eyes.

For once, he let the sky look after itself.
