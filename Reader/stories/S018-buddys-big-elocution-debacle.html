Buddy’s Big Elocution Debacle

10 minute read · 39 illustrations
In this story
  1. The Preparation
  2. The Elocution Championship
  3. The Speech (Disaster)
  4. The Aftermath

After the wild bazaar adventure, Chirag had another brilliant idea.

“Buddy, you’ve come a long way in learning Gujarati,” he said, wagging his tail. “It’s time to put those skills to the test.”

Buddy looked up. He had successfully asked a price. The rest of that lesson had involved pursuing a stolen chapati toward a cow. Chirag’s idea of progress covered a remarkable distance.

“What kind of test?” he asked, suspiciously.

Chirag grinned. “An elocution competition.”

Buddy blinked. “A what now?”

“Public speaking.”

“Could I start with private speaking? To someone asleep?”

Heather clapped excitedly. “Oh, this is perfect! The annual Gujarati Elocution Championship for Neighborhood Pets is happening this weekend. It’ll be a great chance for you to show off your skills!”

Neet snickered from his command post, a particularly regal-looking pile of laundry. “Oh, this is going to be amazing.”

Buddy looked away from Neet. He had laughed through enough of his brother’s mistakes. He wanted to find out whether he could do better when it was his turn.

“How short can a speech be?” he asked.

“We’ll write one you can practice,” Chirag said.

Buddy considered the laundry audience. At least nobody was waving a rolling pin.

“All right. But he doesn’t get to help.”

Neet rose. “I’m the audience.”

That sounded harmless. Buddy would remember thinking so.

The Preparation

The next few days were a blur of preparation. Chirag insisted on rigorous practice, drilling Buddy with common phrases and refining his pronunciation.

“Say, ‘Aaje tapman ochhu chhe.’” (Today the temperature is low.)

Buddy cleared his throat. “Aaaahje top man ochu chhe.”

Chirag sighed. “No, no, tap-man, not top man. You’re not discussing superhero rankings.”

“I’d watch a movie called Top Man Ochu Chhe,” Neet said. Buddy waited for him to finish, then tried the phrase again.

Neet opened his mouth to offer another review. Buddy calmly turned his practice page around so its blank back faced him.

“The audience,” Buddy said, “is having a silent rehearsal.”

Neet sat taller. He could be magnificent at silence. For nearly four seconds, he was.

Then he noticed the subject of the speech.

“Food?”

“Food and its cultural significance,” Chirag said.

“Excellent. I’ll judge the food.”

“There isn’t any.”

Neet looked at the blank page. It had begun to seem suspiciously accurate.

Buddy chose the short speech Chirag helped him prepare. He knew what he wanted to say about people gathering over good food; knowing how to keep its unfamiliar words in order was another matter. He practiced one section at a time, with Chirag stopping him when he lost his way.

Then Buddy made a rule: if he went wrong, he would start from the beginning.

It seemed sensible until Neet applauded the first mention of dhokla.

Buddy stopped.

“What?” Neet asked. “It’s very good.”

Buddy started again. Neet applauded again.

Chirag watched the third attempt. “We’ve heard your opening. Can we hear what happens after it?”

“I don’t know,” Buddy said. “Dhokla keeps happening.”

Heather set down her laundry and sat opposite him. When Neet drew breath to contribute, she rested a hand on his head.

“Let him finish.”

Buddy tried once more. He stumbled, looked at Chirag, and began to retreat toward his first sentence.

Heather waited. Chirag pointed his nose toward the next line instead.

Buddy found it. Then another. Neet stayed beneath Heather’s hand until the end, wearing the expression of someone being deprived of several valuable opinions.

Despite the struggles, Buddy gave it his best. By the time competition day arrived, he had memorized a short but solid speech about Gujarati food and its cultural significance. He could deliver it at home, provided the dhokla passed without a celebration.

He was ready. Well… mostly.

The Elocution Championship

The event was set in a small park, complete with a raised platform, colorful banners, and a microphone that stood just a little too tall for Buddy.

Heather arrived holding both dogs’ leashes. Buddy walked beside her knee; Neet led by as much slack as she allowed, inspecting a gathering he hadn’t organized.

“Who’s in charge?” he asked.

Chirag nodded toward the judges’ table.

Neet surveyed it. “Several of them. Inefficient.”

Buddy looked at the rows of chairs. His home audience had been two dogs and Heather. This audience appeared to come with spare audience.

He asked to see the stage before his turn. Heather took him to its side steps and unclipped his leash while keeping Neet’s loop in her hand. She remained beside the steps with the folded speech.

Buddy climbed up. The microphone looked easier to address from the grass, where it hadn’t been pointed at him.

A volunteer lowered it. Buddy leaned in for a cautious test.

His small throat-clearing emerged from the speaker behind the chairs as a much more ambitious throat-clearing.

Buddy turned sharply.

Neet barked at the speaker. “He’s already doing it!”

Heather drew Neet closer. Buddy looked from the microphone to the distant sound, then tried again without turning around. The second cough was equally public. At least it stayed where he expected it.

He returned down the steps, and Heather clipped his leash back on while they waited.

“Good,” Chirag said. “Now you know.”

Buddy had been happier not knowing.

The competition began with a parrot named Mithu reciting a flawless Gujarati poem about mangoes. The audience oohed and aahed at his diction. Buddy quietly crossed mangoes off the list of subjects he hoped nobody else would mention.

He tried following his own speech while Mithu spoke. The parrot’s voice and the words on Buddy’s page soon occupied the same crowded space in his head.

Buddy looked up. The judges were listening. Nobody was checking his practice sheet. He pushed it back toward Heather and watched Mithu finish instead.

Next, a Persian cat named Rani delivered a dramatic monologue on the beauty of the Gujarati monsoon, complete with exaggerated paw gestures.

Buddy raised a paw experimentally.

Neet leaned over. “Are you feeling rain?”

Buddy put it down. He had enough trouble remembering what to say without also remembering what his feet meant.

Rani left the stage at the speed of someone expecting an encore. Passing Buddy, she paused to straighten a whisker.

“Are you doing gestures?”

“I’m doing words,” Buddy said. “They’re taking up the whole appointment.”

Then his name was called.

Buddy’s paws stayed on the grass. For a moment he considered letting the name belong to some other Buddy.

Heather crouched beside him. She held the paper where he could see the beginning, then folded it without rushing him.

“You got this.”

Buddy looked toward the side steps. He could reach those. He stood and went with her.

Heather unclipped his leash there, put the speech within reach on the podium, and returned to the front row. Neet and Chirag sat beside her, wagging their tails expectantly. Neet’s leash still ended in her hand.

Then it was Buddy’s turn. Nobody had lowered the microphone for courage.

The Speech (Disaster)

Buddy climbed onto the podium, shifted closer to the lowered microphone, and took a deep breath.

“Namaste, mara mitro!” (Hello, my friends!)

The audience clapped encouragingly.

Buddy smiled nervously. This was louder than Neet’s dhokla celebration. He waited for it to finish, and in the waiting, the next sentence moved somewhere inconvenient.

He looked down and continued. “Gujarati khorak… tamaru swagat kare chhe.” (Gujarati food… welcomes you?)

Chirag smacked his forehead.

Buddy swallowed. The beginning was still available. He could go back and welcome everybody again. They were already here, though. He tried to find what came next.

“Dhokla… ek saro… motor cycle chhe.” (Dhokla is a good… motorcycle?)

Neet had to bite his paw to keep from howling in laughter.

The audience exchanged puzzled looks, but Buddy bravely pushed forward.

“Sev tamari saathe… prem kare chhe.” (Sev loves you.)

Chirag groaned. “Oh no.”

Buddy looked down at his page. He knew all these words. They had simply stopped standing next to the right neighbors.

Neet was in tears. He lifted his head as if he might offer a suggestion. Heather’s hand came down again. This time he stayed seated without a complaint.

Buddy heard somebody chuckle. His ears warmed. He had laughed at Neet for saying cheese toast; now his own food had acquired transport and feelings.

There was still a clear path to the side steps. He glanced toward Heather. She wasn’t beckoning him down. She sat where he could see her, smiling, with his disconnected leash folded in her lap.

Buddy faced the microphone again.

Realizing he was losing control, he blurted out the first thing that came to mind.

“Mango ras bahu shakti shali chhe!” (Mango juice is very powerful!)

That, for some reason, got a huge round of applause.

Neet stopped trying to laugh quietly and joined in the celebration. It was possible, apparently, to enjoy a food speech without being served anything.

A few people laughed, but they kept applauding. Buddy looked at the page he could no longer follow. He could stop there, or finish. He raised his head.

Buddy, now extremely confused, wagged his tail and gave a polite bow. “Uh… Jai Gujarat?”

This time, when the applause came, he let it finish without searching for another sentence.

The Aftermath

Heather met him at the side steps, collected his speech, and clipped his leash on again. Buddy pressed against her leg before following her behind the platform.

Backstage, Heather laid the speech beside him. Neet tried to repeat “motorcycle dhokla” and had to stop laughing long enough to breathe. Buddy waited, holding the edge of his speech beneath one paw.

Chirag shook his head. “That was… something.”

Buddy sighed, tail drooping. “I completely butchered it.”

Heather, wiping away tears of laughter, hugged him. “You were amazing! I mean, I have no idea what you said, but you owned that stage.”

Chirag looked at the page. “We can practice the words again.”

“Tomorrow,” Buddy said firmly.

Neet began another attempt at motorcycle dhokla. Buddy turned the page over and slid its blank back toward him.

Neet recognized it. He sat down.

Before anybody could test his new silence, the prize announcements began. Heather gathered the folded speech and led them back around the platform.

To Buddy’s astonishment, the judges awarded him the ‘Most Enthusiastic Speaker’ prize!

Mithu and Rani received their own applause, and Buddy joined in before Heather brought him forward to accept his small golden paw-shaped trophy.

Neet leaned over and whispered, “So, should we start working on your speech for next year?”

Buddy groaned. “Absolutely not.”

Neet studied the trophy. “What did you say to win that?”

“For once,” Buddy said, “you were listening. You tell me.”

Neet opened his mouth. Closed it.

Heather lowered a congratulatory hand onto Buddy’s head. “There. Two prizes.”

She carried the trophy and speech home. After unclipping both dogs’ leashes by the door, she put the paper by Buddy’s usual resting spot and lowered the trophy within reach.

He set the little trophy beside his folded speech. The words had gone astray, but he had stayed at the microphone until the end.

Neet sat beside him without offering a single suggestion. Buddy left the trophy where they could both see it.
