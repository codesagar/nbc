# Neet

A small, tall-looking tan dog with long fine legs, a real but narrow torso, a visible neck, large broad triangular ears and an extremely short tail nub. Preserve the space beneath his body. Lean does not mean exposed ribs, a hollow waist or a needle-thin trunk. The smooth, very short golden/fawn coat has a cream muzzle, chin, throat and irregular chest bib; the nose and lip rims are dark. No long tail, plume, uniform high white socks or hard black saddle is established.

His head is modest beside the body. Use the gently rounded forehead and clearly projecting, tapering muzzle rather than a round puppy head, flat button face or fox-like snout. Brown eyes have dark rims; white sclera appears with strong gaze shifts rather than as a default feature. Ears are broad-rooted, cupped triangles with pointed tips and variable root angles, not narrow rabbit ears. N35's folded ears are an unresolved outlier and must not establish anatomy.

## Personality and story voice

The personality reference describes cozy, introverted rest followed by sudden alertness and comically oversized confidence. “General Neet” reads as cute seriousness. He curls into beds or den-like spaces, listens with small head tilts, sits unusually upright, sometimes crosses his paws, stretches chest-down with his rump raised, yawns dramatically and relaxes in goofy belly-up shapes. Squeaky-toy interest is a written personality claim; photos show only visible toy contact. Tiny rapid stub motion likewise comes from prose, not a verified frame sequence.

Narrative guidance stresses impulsive, fearless overreaction, self-appointed security patrols and affectionate frustration from Buddy. Use that energy for the plot while preserving space for comfort and vulnerability. A production brief should state which interpretation a conflicting scene uses. Nicknames such as “General Neet,” “4.9 star general,” Buddy's “lunatic,” and Sagar's affectionate “idiot” work sparingly and contextually.
